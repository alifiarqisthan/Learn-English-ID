## Explanation

The **past perfect** expresses an action that was **completed before another
action in the past**. It answers the question: *which of these two past events
happened first?*

> "When I arrived, she **had already left**." — she left first, then I arrived.
> "He **had studied** for months before he took the exam." — studying came before the exam.
> "By the time we got to the cinema, the film **had started**." — film started first.

Think of it as the "earlier past" — a past-in-the-past. The timeline has two
points, and the past perfect marks the **earlier** one.

```
[earlier event]        [later event]
had + V3    ←    before / by the time / when    →    simple past
```

> Compare with [Simple Past →](/modules/simple-past) for the "later" event, and
> [Past Continuous →](/modules/past-continuous) for actions ongoing at a past moment.

### When to use it

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Earlier of two past events | She **had eaten** before she went out. |
| Result relevant at a past moment | I was exhausted — I **hadn't slept** all night. |
| Reporting past experience up to a past point | He **had never visited** Jakarta before that trip. |
| After "by the time", "when", "before", "after" | **By the time** the team arrived, the match **had already ended**. |
| Unfulfilled past expectation | I **had hoped** to see her, but she didn't come. |

### Form — had + V3

All subjects use the **same auxiliary**: **had**. Then the past participle (**V3**) of the main verb.

#### Positive

```
subject + had + V3
```

| Subject | Auxiliary | V3 | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / You / He / She / It / We / They | **had** | finished | I **had finished** the work. |

> No subject changes anything here — *had* is the same for every subject.

Common irregular V3 forms you need for past perfect:

| V1 | V3 |
|---|---|
| go | gone |
| eat | eaten |
| see | seen |
| write | written |
| take | taken |
| give | given |
| speak | spoken |
| do | done |
| make | made |
| know | known |
| leave | left |
| lose | lost |
| buy | bought |
| think | thought |
| begin | begun |
| fall | fallen |
| break | broken |

> For the full list see [Top 100 Irregular Verbs →](/references/irregular-verbs).

#### Negative

```
subject + had + not + V3
→ hadn't + V3
```

> I **hadn't eaten** anything all day.
> She **hadn't told** anyone about the news.
> They **hadn't been** to that restaurant before.

#### Question

```
Had + subject + V3 ?
Wh-word + had + subject + V3 ?
```

> **Had** you **finished** the report before the meeting?
> **Had** she **ever visited** Paris before that trip?
> **Where had** he **gone** before he came home?
> **Why hadn't** they **called**?

### Signal words — the most important clue

These words almost always signal that past perfect is needed for the **earlier** event:

| Signal word/phrase | Example |
|---|---|
| **by the time** | **By the time** he arrived, I **had already left**. |
| **before** | She **had finished** her degree **before** she got the job. |
| **after** (the clause before "after") | **After** he **had read** the report, he made a decision. |
| **when** (the earlier event) | **When** I arrived, she **had been** waiting for an hour. |
| **already** | They **had already eaten** by 6pm. |
| **just** | She **had just left** when I called. |
| **never … before** | I **had never tasted** durian before that day. |
| **yet** (in negatives) | He **hadn't finished** yet when the time was up. |

### Past Perfect vs Simple Past — the key distinction

This is the most common confusion at B1 level. Both are past tenses, but they describe **different positions on the timeline**.

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| When I arrived, she **left**. | She left at the moment I arrived (almost simultaneously). |
| When I arrived, she **had left**. | She left before I arrived — she was already gone. |

> "I **ate** breakfast and went to school." — ate, then went (sequential, simple past)
> "I **had eaten** breakfast before I went to school." — eating was finished before going started

Use **past perfect** when you want to make it unambiguous that one past action was **already complete** before the second one began.

### "After" and "before" — do you always need past perfect?

With *before* and *after*, the sequence is already clear from the word itself,
so both simple past and past perfect are often acceptable:

> "She **left** before I arrived." ✅ (simple past — *before* makes the order clear)
> "She **had left** before I arrived." ✅ (past perfect — explicitly marks the earlier event)

Both are grammatically correct. Past perfect adds **emphasis** to the completeness.

With *when*, however, the two tenses have **different meanings** (as shown above), so the choice matters more.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### No "earlier past" tense in Indonesian

Bahasa Indonesia does not have a grammatical structure dedicated to "earlier
past." The same sentence can describe something that happened before or after
another past event — you rely entirely on **context and order words**.

| Indonesian | English |
|---|---|
| Ketika saya tiba, dia sudah pergi. | When I arrived, she **had already left**. |
| Sebelum dia pergi, dia sudah makan. | Before she left, she **had eaten**. |
| Setelah dia **menyelesaikan** laporannya, dia pulang. | After she **had finished** her report, she went home. |
| Dia belum pernah ke Jakarta sebelum itu. | She **had never been** to Jakarta before that. |

Indonesian uses **sudah** (already/already done) and **belum** (not yet) to signal completion, but these map to present perfect in most contexts — when the time frame is past-before-past, English uses past perfect.

### The "sudah" trap — past perfect vs simple past vs present perfect

This is the central confusion for Indonesian learners:

| Indonesian | Which English tense? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Saya sudah makan. (now) | Present perfect: *I have eaten.* | Completed, result relevant now |
| Saya sudah makan tadi. (earlier today, specific time) | Simple past: *I ate earlier.* | Specific past time |
| Saya sudah makan sebelum dia datang. (before another past event) | Past perfect: *I had eaten before she arrived.* | Earlier of two past events |

The rule: when *sudah* appears in a sentence that already has **another past event**, and the *sudah* action came **first**, use **past perfect**.

### Side-by-side: the 4 past tenses mapped to Indonesian

| Indonesian | English tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Saya makan (kemarin) | Simple Past | I **ate** yesterday. |
| Saya **sedang** makan ketika dia datang | Past Continuous | I **was eating** when she arrived. |
| Saya **sudah** makan sebelum dia datang | Past Perfect | I **had eaten** before she arrived. |
| Saya **sudah makan selama** 2 jam ketika dia datang (masih berlangsung) | Past Perfect Continuous | I **had been eating** for 2 hours when she arrived. |

### Translation strategy

1. **Find both past events.** Which one happened first?
2. **The earlier event → had + V3.** The later event → simple past.
3. **Check signal words:** *sebelum/setelah/sudah…sebelum/ketika* → these usually signal which event is earlier.
4. **Check for "belum pernah"** (had never) → had + never + V3.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using simple past for both events when order matters**
- ❌ When I arrived, she **left**. *(implies she left at the same moment I arrived)*
- ✅ When I arrived, she **had already left**. *(she was gone before I got there)*

  *Why it happens:* Indonesian doesn't grammatically distinguish the two — context does the work. In English, "had left" is the only unambiguous way to say "she left before I arrived."

**2. Using present perfect instead of past perfect**
- ❌ By the time we arrived, they **have eaten** all the food.
- ✅ By the time we arrived, they **had eaten** all the food.

  *Why it happens:* present perfect (*have eaten*) is about "now." Past perfect (*had eaten*) is about "before a past moment." If you're already in the past, you can't use present perfect.

**3. Forgetting the irregular V3 form**
- ❌ She **had went** home already.
- ❌ He **had ate** before we arrived.
- ✅ She **had gone** home already.
- ✅ He **had eaten** before we arrived.

  *Why it happens:* V3 (past participle) and V2 (past simple) are different for many irregular verbs. *went* is V2; *gone* is V3. After *had*, you always need V3.

**4. Using "did" with past perfect**
- ❌ She **didn't had** finished the work.
- ✅ She **hadn't finished** the work.

  *Why it happens:* over-applying the *did/didn't* rule. Like *was/were* for past continuous, *had* makes its own negatives — never use *did* with past perfect.

**5. Overusing past perfect where sequence is already clear**
- ❌ I **had brushed** my teeth and then I **had gone** to bed.
- ✅ I **brushed** my teeth and then **went** to bed.

  *Why it happens:* once learners discover past perfect, they use it everywhere. If you have *and then*, *first…then*, the order is already clear — just use simple past.

**6. Wrong word order with "already"**
- ❌ They **had eaten already** all the food.
- ✅ They **had already eaten** all the food.

  *Position of "already":* between *had* and *V3*.

## Quick Summary

- Form: **had + V3** (same for all subjects — no had/has split).
- Use for: the **earlier** of two past events — the "past-in-the-past."
- Key signals: **by the time, before, after, when** (with two past events), **already, just, never … before**.
- Negative: **hadn't + V3**. Never use *didn't* with past perfect.
- Question: **Had + subject + V3?** No *did*.
- V3 ≠ V2: *went* (V2) vs *gone* (V3), *ate* (V2) vs *eaten* (V3), *wrote* (V2) vs *written* (V3).
- Indonesian *sudah…sebelum/ketika* (earlier of two past events) → **had + V3**.
- Don't overuse: if *and then* / *first…then* already shows the order, simple past is enough.
