## Explanation

The **simple past** describes a completed action at a specific time in the past.
The action started and finished — there is no connection to "now."

> "I **walked** to the market yesterday." — finished. Done. In the past.

This is one of the **most important tenses in English**, and the one Indonesian
learners struggle with the most, because Bahasa Indonesia has **no verb
conjugation for time**. We need to slow down and learn it carefully.

### V1 and V2 — the two forms we need

For the simple past, every verb has two forms we care about:

| Form | Name | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **V1** | Base form (the dictionary form) | Present simple, after "to", **after did/didn't** |
| **V2** | Past simple form | The simple past — this whole module |

> Some verbs have a third form (V3) used in other tenses. For the full picture
> see [Verb Forms Guide →](/references/verb-forms). For now, focus only on
> **V1 → V2**.

There are two kinds of verbs:

- **Regular verbs** — V2 = V1 + **-ed** (walk → walked, play → played).
- **Irregular verbs** — V2 is a special form you must memorize (go → went, write → wrote).

### Forms (positive, negative, question)

#### Positive

```
subject + V2
```

| Subject | V2 | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | walked / went | They **walked** home. They **went** home. |
| He / She / It | walked / went | She **walked** home. She **went** home. |

The V2 form is **the same for every subject**. No -s, no -es.

> I **went** to school. (not *I goed*)
> She **wrote** a letter. (not *She writed*)

#### Negative

```
subject + did not / didn't + V1
```

> I **did not eat** breakfast.
> They **didn't see** the movie.
> She **didn't go** to school. *(not "didn't went")*

⚠️ After **did/didn't**, the verb returns to **V1** — the past meaning is
already carried by *did*.

#### Question

```
Did + subject + V1 ?
Wh-word + did + subject + V1 ?
```

> **Did** you **finish** the homework?
> **Where did** she **go**? *(not "where did she went")*
> **Why didn't** they **come**?

### Spelling rules for regular -ed

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Most verbs: just add **-ed** | walk → walk**ed**, play → play**ed** |
| Verb ends in **-e**: add only **-d** | live → live**d**, dance → dance**d** |
| Consonant + **y**: change y to **i** + **-ed** | study → stud**ied**, try → tr**ied** |
| Vowel + **y**: just add **-ed** | play → play**ed**, enjoy → enjoy**ed** |
| One syllable, CVC pattern: **double** the consonant | stop → sto**pped**, plan → pla**nned** |
| Two syllables, stress on last: double the consonant | prefer → prefer**red**, admit → admi**tted** |
| Two syllables, stress on first: do **NOT** double | open → opene**d**, listen → listene**d** |

### Pronunciation of -ed (often tested in TOEFL listening)

The spelling is always *-ed*, but the **sound** depends on the last sound of V1:

| Last sound of V1 | -ed sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **/t/** or **/d/** | **/ɪd/** (extra syllable) | want**ed** /wɒntɪd/, need**ed** /niːdɪd/ |
| Voiceless: /p/ /k/ /f/ /s/ /ʃ/ /tʃ/ | **/t/** | wal**ked** /wɔːkt/, sto**pped** /stɒpt/ |
| All other (voiced + vowels) | **/d/** | play**ed** /pleɪd/, lov**ed** /lʌvd/ |

### Irregular V2 forms — by pattern

About 200 English verbs are irregular, and **the most common verbs are all
irregular**. You can't talk about the past without memorizing their V2 forms.

There's no rule that always works — but irregulars **do** fall into patterns.
Learning them by pattern is much faster than learning each one alone.

> For the full reference list of the 100 most common irregular verbs (with V3 forms too), see [Top 100 Irregular Verbs →](/references/irregular-verbs).

#### Pattern 1 — No change (V1 = V2)

| V1 | V2 |
|---|---|
| cut | cut |
| put | put |
| hit | hit |
| let | let |
| set | set |
| shut | shut |
| read /riːd/ | read /red/ |

> Yesterday I **cut** my finger. *(spelling unchanged, but it's still simple past)*

For *read*, the spelling stays the same but the pronunciation changes from
/riːd/ to /red/.

#### Pattern 5 — Totally irregular (memorize first)

These are the three most common verbs in English, and they don't follow any pattern. Learn them before anything else.

| V1 | V2 |
|---|---|
| **be** | **was / were** |
| **go** | **went** |
| **do** | **did** |

### "to be" in the past — was / were

**This is the section Indonesian learners need most.** In Bahasa Indonesia,
the verb "to be" is usually **dropped entirely** ("*Saya lelah*" — no copula),
but in English you cannot leave it out. You must say "I **am** tired" (present)
or "I **was** tired" (past).

> For the complete picture of "to be" across all tenses (present, past, future)
> and forms, see [Verb "to be" Master Guide →](/references/verb-to-be).

Worse, "to be" is the only English verb with **two past forms** depending on
the subject — and it doesn't follow the normal *did* pattern.

#### Two forms, picked by subject

| Subject | Past of "to be" |
|---|---|
| I | **was** |
| He / She / It | **was** |
| You (singular & plural) | **were** |
| We | **were** |
| They | **were** |

Memory trick: **only "I" and singular he/she/it use *was*.** Everything else uses *were*.

#### Positive

```
subject + was/were + (rest)
```

> I **was** tired last night.
> She **was** at the office.
> They **were** late for the meeting.
> You **were** my best student.

#### Negative

```
subject + was/were + not  →  was not / wasn't, were not / weren't
```

> I **was not** ready. / I **wasn't** ready.
> He **wasn't** at home.
> They **were not** invited. / They **weren't** invited.

⚠️ Do **not** use *did/didn't* with "to be":
- ❌ He **didn't was** ready.
- ✅ He **wasn't** ready.

#### Question

```
Was / Were + subject + (rest) ?
Wh-word + was/were + subject + (rest) ?
```

> **Was** he angry?
> **Were** you at the party?
> **Where were** they yesterday?
> **Why was** she late?

⚠️ Same rule — no *did* in questions either:
- ❌ **Did** you **were** at home?
- ✅ **Were** you at home?

#### Side-by-side: regular verbs vs "to be"

| Form | Regular verb (e.g. *go*) | "to be" |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | I **went** home. | I **was** home. |
| Negative | I **did not go** home. | I **was not** home. |
| Question | **Did** you **go** home? | **Were** you home? |

The right column needs **no auxiliary** — was/were do all the work themselves.

#### Why this trips up Indonesian speakers

Bahasa Indonesia almost always **drops** "to be":

| Bahasa Indonesia | English (must include was/were) |
|---|---|
| Saya **lelah** kemarin. | I **was** tired yesterday. |
| Dia **di rumah** tadi malam. | She **was** at home last night. |
| Mereka **siswa** saya tahun lalu. | They **were** my students last year. |
| **Dingin** kemarin. | It **was** cold yesterday. |

There is **no Indonesian word** in those sentences that translates to *was*
or *were* — the copula simply isn't there. Indonesian learners often write
"*I tired yesterday*" or "*She at home last night*" because they're
translating word-for-word.

The fix: every time you write a past sentence in English without an action verb,
**ask yourself "do I need was or were here?"**

### Common past time markers

These signal that the simple past is needed:

- **yesterday**, the day before yesterday
- **last** night / week / month / year / Friday
- **ago**: two days ago, three years ago, a long time ago
- **in** + past year: in 2010, in 1995
- **when I was a child / young / in school**
- **then**, **at that time**, **back then**

> *Three years ago* I **moved** to Jakarta. *When I was 10*, I **could not** speak English.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### The fundamental difference

In Bahasa Indonesia, **verbs do not change form for tense**. Time is expressed
through **context words** instead of verb conjugation. This single fact is the
root cause of nearly every Simple Past mistake Indonesian learners make.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English (Simple Past) |
|---|---|
| Kemarin saya **makan** nasi. | Yesterday I **ate** rice. |
| Tahun lalu dia **pergi** ke Bali. | Last year he **went** to Bali. |
| Saya **menulis** surat tadi pagi. | I **wrote** a letter this morning. |
| Mereka **tidur** lebih awal. | They **slept** earlier. |

The Indonesian verbs **makan**, **pergi**, **menulis**, **tidur** stay
**exactly the same** whether you're talking about today, yesterday, or ten
years ago. The time marker (*kemarin*, *tahun lalu*, *tadi pagi*) carries
all the temporal meaning by itself.

In English, **both** the time marker **and** the verb form must change.
This is a double burden Indonesian learners aren't used to.

### Why irregular V2 feels unnatural to us

Bahasa Indonesia has affixes (`me-`, `ber-`, `di-`) but they change the
**voice** or **role** of the verb, not the **time**:

- **menulis** (to write) — active
- **ditulis** (to be written) — passive
- **menuliskan** (to write something for someone) — applicative

Notice: **none of these tell you when** the writing happened. That's done by
context words like *kemarin*, *sudah*, *tadi*, *baru saja*.

So when an Indonesian learner sees *write → wrote* or *go → went*, it feels
**arbitrary**. Why do English speakers change the *verb itself* for time?
The honest answer: it's just how the language evolved. There's no Indonesian
equivalent that maps onto it — you have to **memorize** the irregular V2 forms.

### "Sudah" and "telah" — a major trap

Indonesian has **sudah** and **telah** ("already"), which feel like they should
match English past tense. But they actually map closer to **present perfect**
(have/has + a special verb form), which is a different tense covered in a later
module.

| Indonesian | What it usually maps to in English |
|---|---|
| **Saya makan kemarin.** | Simple past: *I ate yesterday.* |
| **Saya sudah makan.** | Present perfect: *I have eaten.* (we'll cover this later) |
| **Saya sudah makan kemarin.** | Simple past: *I ate yesterday.* (specific time = simple past wins) |

The rule of thumb: **specific past time → simple past**. *Sudah/telah* alone
without a specific time → usually present perfect.

### Side-by-side: what changes, what doesn't

| Feature | Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|---|
| Verb form changes for time? | ❌ Never | ✅ Yes — V1 → V2 |
| Subject affects verb form? | ❌ Never | Mostly no — except *to be* (was/were) |
| Negation in past | tidak / belum + verb | did not + V1 (or was/were not) |
| Question word order | "Apakah kamu...?" | "Did you...?" / "Were you...?" |
| Special verb for "to be" past | ❌ No (often dropped) | ✅ was / were |

### Translation strategy (use this every time)

When translating an Indonesian sentence into English simple past, ask:

1. **Is there a past time marker?** (kemarin, tadi, minggu lalu, dulu) → simple past is needed.
2. **What's the main verb?** Find V1, then convert to V2 (regular -ed, or irregular).
3. **Is it positive, negative, or a question?**
   - Positive: subject + V2
   - Negative: subject + did not + V1 (or wasn't/weren't for "to be")
   - Question: Did + subject + V1? (or Was/Were + subject?)
4. **Is the verb "to be"?** Then use was/were directly — no "did".

## Common Mistakes

These are the patterns I see most often from Indonesian learners. They all
trace back to one root cause: **Bahasa Indonesia doesn't conjugate verbs for time**.

**1. Using V1 with a past time marker**
- ❌ Yesterday I **go** to school.
- ✅ Yesterday I **went** to school.

  *Why it happens:* "kemarin saya pergi" — the Indonesian verb doesn't change,
  so the learner forgets to change V1 to V2 in English.

**2. Adding -ed to irregular verbs**
- ❌ He **goed** home. / I **eated** lunch. / She **writed** a letter.
- ✅ He **went** home. / I **ate** lunch. / She **wrote** a letter.

  *Why it happens:* the -ed rule is overgeneralized to verbs that have their
  own irregular V2 form. Memorize the irregular V2s.

**3. Using V2 after "did"**
- ❌ Did you **went** to the store?
- ❌ She **didn't ate** breakfast.
- ✅ Did you **go** to the store?
- ✅ She **didn't eat** breakfast.

  *Why it happens:* once "did" carries the past meaning, the main verb returns
  to **V1** (base form). Indonesian has no equivalent rule, so this feels redundant.

**4. Confusing simple past with present perfect**
- ❌ I **have eaten** rice yesterday.
- ✅ I **ate** rice yesterday.

  *Why it happens:* both translate to "saya sudah makan" or "saya makan kemarin"
  in Indonesian. With a **specific past time**, English requires simple past.

**5. Forgetting "was/were" for the verb "to be"**
- ❌ I **be** tired last night. / She **is** at home yesterday.
- ❌ He **didn't was** ready.
- ✅ I **was** tired last night. / She **was** at home yesterday.
- ✅ He **wasn't** ready.

  *Why it happens:* in Indonesian, the verb "to be" is often **dropped**
  (*"Saya lelah tadi malam"* — no copula). So learners either omit it or
  treat it like a normal verb with "did".

**6. Wrong subject-verb agreement with was/were**
- ❌ They **was** at school. / We **was** late.
- ✅ They **were** at school. / We **were** late.

  *Why it happens:* Indonesian doesn't change verb form by subject, so
  picking between *was* and *were* feels random.

**7. Spelling errors with -ed**
- ❌ studyed, stoped, livved
- ✅ studied, stopped, lived

  *Why it happens:* the spelling rules (consonant doubling, y → i) aren't
  obvious. Memorize the rules above and watch for the patterns.
