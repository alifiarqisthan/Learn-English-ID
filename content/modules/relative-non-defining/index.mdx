## Explanation

A **non-defining relative clause** (also called a **non-restrictive relative clause**) adds **extra, non-essential information** about a noun. The noun is already fully identified without the clause — the clause is a bonus remark.

> My brother, **who lives in London**, is a doctor.

Remove the clause and the sentence is still complete and clear:
- "My brother is a doctor." ✅ *(still makes sense)*

Compare with a defining clause where removing it changes or destroys the meaning:
> The student **who passed** got a certificate. *(removing "who passed" → "The student got a certificate" — which student?)*

### The comma rule — mandatory

Non-defining clauses are **always** set off with **commas** (or dashes in informal writing). The commas signal "this is parenthetical information."

- ✅ My sister, **who is a lawyer**, lives in Surabaya.
- ❌ My sister **who is a lawyer** lives in Surabaya. *(defines which sister — implies you have more than one)*

### "That" is NOT used in non-defining clauses

This is a hard rule. Only **who, whom, which, whose, where, when** — never "that."

| Defining | Non-defining |
|---|---|
| The book **that** I recommended is a bestseller. | *My first novel,* **which** *I published in 2010, is out of print.* |
| The scientist **that** discovered penicillin... | *Alexander Fleming,* **who** *discovered penicillin, was Scottish.* |

### Summary of differences

| Feature | Defining | Non-defining |
|---|---|---|
| Essential to meaning? | Yes | No |
| Commas? | No | Yes — always |
| "that" allowed? | Yes | No |
| Changes sentence meaning if removed? | Yes | No |

### When is a clause non-defining?

Non-defining clauses typically appear after:

1. **Proper nouns** (names): *London, which is the capital, …* / *Dr Santos, who leads the team, …*
2. **Unique nouns** (only one exists): *The sun, which is a star, …* / *My mother, who …*
3. **Possessives that already identify**: *Her new car, which she bought last month, …*

### "Which" referring to a whole clause

In non-defining clauses, **which** can refer to the entire previous clause, not just a noun. This is very common in academic writing.

> She finished the project on time, **which surprised everyone**. *(which = the fact that she finished on time)*
> The experiment failed, **which led to a major revision of the methodology**. *(which = the experiment failing)*

⚠️ "That" cannot be used this way:
- ❌ She finished on time, that surprised everyone.
- ✅ She finished on time, **which** surprised everyone.

### Non-defining with "whose", "where", "when"

**Whose:**
> The company, **whose CEO resigned last month**, is undergoing major restructuring.

**Where:**
> The University of Indonesia, **where she studied**, is in Depok.

**When:**
> In 2020, **when the pandemic began**, many businesses closed.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### No comma distinction in Indonesian

Bahasa Indonesia does not grammatically distinguish defining from non-defining relative clauses. Both use **yang** — and Indonesian punctuation is more flexible. This means Indonesian learners must consciously **choose** the right clause type in English.

| Bahasa Indonesia | Defining (no comma) | Non-defining (comma) |
|---|---|---|
| Adik saya yang tinggal di London adalah dokter. | My sister **who lives in London** is a doctor. *(implies more than one sister)* | My sister, **who lives in London**, is a doctor. *(only one sister)* |
| Buku yang dia tulis sangat populer. | The book **that she wrote** is very popular. *(specific book)* | *Her book,* **which** *she wrote in 2015, is very popular.* *(only one book)* |

The comma choice completely changes the meaning in English — and there is **no equivalent distinction in Indonesian**. This is one of the most important things to learn.

### "Which" referring to a whole clause — no Indonesian parallel

Indonesian uses *hal ini* (this matter / this fact) or just context. English uses "which" after a comma to refer back:

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Dia lulus dengan nilai sempurna. Hal ini mengejutkan semua orang. | She graduated with a perfect score, **which** surprised everyone. |
| Eksperimen itu gagal. Hal itu memaksa mereka merevisi metodologi. | The experiment failed, **which** forced them to revise the methodology. |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Missing commas around non-defining clause**
- ❌ My brother who is a doctor lives in Bali.
- ✅ My brother, **who** is a doctor, lives in Bali.

  *Why:* without commas, this implies you have more than one brother and you are identifying which one.

**2. Using "that" in a non-defining clause**
- ❌ Jakarta, **that** is the capital of Indonesia, has a population of over 10 million.
- ✅ Jakarta, **which** is the capital of Indonesia, has a population of over 10 million.

**3. Using "which" for a whole clause without a comma**
- ❌ She passed the exam which surprised her family.
- ✅ She passed the exam**, which** surprised her family. *(comma required when "which" refers to the whole clause)*

**4. Confusing "whose" and "who's"**
- ❌ The director, who's film won an award, will speak today.
- ✅ The director, **whose** film won an award, will speak today.

**5. Using only one comma instead of two**
- ❌ My sister, who is a lawyer lives in Surabaya.
- ✅ My sister**,** who is a lawyer**,** lives in Surabaya.

  *Both commas are required — before and after the non-defining clause.*

**6. Defining vs. non-defining: wrong choice changes meaning**
- "The students **who passed** received diplomas." *(only those who passed — defining)*
- "The students, **who all passed**, received diplomas." *(all students passed — non-defining)*

## Quick Summary

- Non-defining relative clause = **extra information** about an already-identified noun — always use **commas**.
- Defining clause: **no commas** | Non-defining clause: **commas required**.
- **Never use "that"** in non-defining clauses — use **who, which, whose, where, when**.
- **"Which"** after a comma can refer to the **entire preceding clause**, not just a noun.
- Proper nouns and unique references almost always take **non-defining** clauses.
- Both commas are required: one before, one after the clause (unless it ends the sentence).
