## Explanation

A **defining relative clause** (also called a **restrictive relative clause**) gives essential information about a noun. Without it, the sentence would be unclear or incomplete — you wouldn't know **which** person, thing, or place is being talked about.

> The woman **who called you** is waiting outside.
> The book **that I recommended** is now a bestseller.
> This is the city **where I was born**.

Remove the clause and the sentence loses critical meaning:
- "The woman is waiting outside." *(Which woman? We don't know.)*

### No commas in defining relative clauses

Defining clauses are **never** set off with commas. The clause is fused to the noun.

- ✅ The student **who passed** got a certificate.
- ❌ The student, who passed, got a certificate. *(that would be non-defining — different meaning)*

### Relative pronouns

| Pronoun | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | people (subject) | The man **who** lives next door is a doctor. |
| **whom** | people (object) — formal | The professor **whom** I respect most... |
| **which** | things / animals | The report **which** she submitted was excellent. |
| **that** | people OR things | The student **that** passed. / The book **that** I read. |
| **whose** | possession (people/things) | The student **whose** laptop was stolen reported it. |
| **where** | places | The café **where** we met is now closed. |
| **when** | times | The year **when** she graduated was 2020. |
| **why** | reasons | The reason **why** she left is unclear. |

### "Who" vs. "that" for people

Both are correct for people in defining clauses. "That" is more common in speech; "who" is preferred in formal writing.

- The scientist **who** discovered penicillin was Alexander Fleming. *(formal)*
- The scientist **that** discovered penicillin... *(informal/neutral)*

### When can you omit the relative pronoun?

When the relative pronoun is the **object** of the clause (not the subject), you can omit it. This is called a **contact clause**.

| Role | Omittable? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | No | The man **who** called me was polite. *(who = subject of "called")* |
| Object | Yes | The book *(that)* I read was fascinating. *("that" = object of "read")* |

> The book **that** I read... = The book I read... ✅
> The man **who** called... ≠ The man called... ❌ *(changes meaning)*

How to tell: if the relative clause already has its own subject after the pronoun, the pronoun is an object and can be dropped.

- "The book **[that]** I read" → "I" is the subject, "that/book" is the object of "read" → droppable.
- "The man **who** called me" → "who" is the subject of "called" → cannot drop.

### "Whose" — possession

**Whose** shows that something belongs to the antecedent noun.

> The student **whose phone rang** apologized. *(the phone belongs to the student)*
> The company **whose products are recalled** must issue a refund.

Whose can refer to both people and things:
> The building **whose roof collapsed** was demolished. ✅

### "Where" and "when"

**Where** replaces "in which / at which" for places:
> The city **where** she grew up. = The city **in which** she grew up.

**When** replaces "in which / at which" for times:
> The moment **when** I realized the truth. = The moment **at which** I realized the truth.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### "Yang" — the Indonesian relative pronoun

Indonesian uses one word for all relative clauses: **yang**. There is no distinction between who, which, that, whose, where, when — just **yang**.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Orang **yang** menelepon saya adalah dokter. | The person **who** called me is a doctor. |
| Buku **yang** saya baca sangat menarik. | The book *(that)* I read was very interesting. |
| Mahasiswa **yang** laptopnya dicuri melapor ke polisi. | The student **whose** laptop was stolen reported it to the police. |
| Kota **yang** dia lahir di sana... | The city **where** she was born... |

Because Indonesian uses just *yang*, learners must learn to select the correct English pronoun based on **what kind of noun** is being modified.

### Common errors from "yang" overgeneralization

| Error | Correct |
|---|---|
| The book which I read it was great. | The book *(that)* I read was great. *(remove "it")* |
| The person which called me was kind. | The person **who** called me was kind. |
| The city which I was born. | The city **where** I was born. |

The extra "it" / "him" / "her" error comes from Indonesian sentence structure where the pronoun is retained inside the relative clause.

### Translation checklist

When translating *yang* to English:
1. Is the noun a **person**? → use **who** (or that)
2. Is the noun a **thing / place / animal**? → use **which** (or that)
3. Is it showing **possession**? → use **whose**
4. Is the noun a **place**? → use **where**
5. Is the noun a **time**? → use **when**
6. Is the pronoun the **object** of the clause? → you can omit it

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using "which" for people**
- ❌ The doctor **which** treated me was excellent.
- ✅ The doctor **who** treated me was excellent.

**2. Retaining a pronoun inside the clause (double pronoun)**
- ❌ The book that I read **it** was interesting.
- ✅ The book that I read was interesting.

  *Why:* the relative pronoun already *is* the object — adding "it" duplicates it.

**3. Using "who" for things**
- ❌ The report **who** she submitted was excellent.
- ✅ The report **which/that** she submitted was excellent.

**4. Using commas (defining → non-defining)**
- ❌ The students, who passed the exam, got certificates.
- ✅ The students **who** passed the exam got certificates. *(defining — specific group of students)*

**5. "Whose" vs. "who's"**
- ❌ The student **who's** laptop was stolen...
- ✅ The student **whose** laptop was stolen...

  *"Who's" = who is / who has. "Whose" = possession.*

**6. Omitting the pronoun when it's a subject**
- ❌ The man called me yesterday was polite.
- ✅ The man **who** called me yesterday was polite.

## Quick Summary

- Defining relative clauses identify **which** noun you mean — essential, no commas.
- **who** = people (subject) | **whom** = people (object, formal) | **which** = things | **that** = people or things.
- **whose** = possession | **where** = place | **when** = time | **why** = reason.
- Relative pronoun can be **omitted** only when it is the **object** of the relative clause.
- Never add a duplicate pronoun inside the clause (❌ the book that I read **it**).
- "Who" for people, "which" for things — never swap them.
- Defining clause = **no commas**. (Commas → non-defining → different meaning.)
