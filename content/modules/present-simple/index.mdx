## Explanation

The **present simple** describes things that are **always true, generally true, or repeated**. It's the tense you use most often in real conversation.

> "I **work** at a hospital." — true now and generally.
> "The sun **rises** in the east." — universal fact.
> "She **drinks** coffee every morning." — repeated routine.

Despite its name, the present simple is **rarely about "right now"** — that's
what the present continuous is for ("I **am working** right now"). The present
simple is for the **bigger picture** of your life and the world.

> Need to compare with another tense? See [Sentence Structure Formulas →](/references/sentence-structure).

### When to use it

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Habits & routines | I **brush** my teeth every morning. |
| Facts & general truths | Water **boils** at 100°C. |
| Permanent states | She **lives** in Bandung. |
| Schedules & timetables | The train **leaves** at 7am. |
| Opinions, preferences, feelings (mental states) | I **like** chocolate. He **knows** the answer. |
| Instructions & directions | First, you **press** this button. |

### Forms — the third-person -s

The thing that makes Present Simple *not* simple for Indonesian learners is
**one rule**: when the subject is **he, she, or it** (or a singular noun), the
verb adds **-s** or **-es**.

| Subject | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | work (V1) | They **work** here. |
| **He / She / It** | works (V1 + **-s**) | She **works** here. |

That's the entire trick. Forget the -s and the sentence sounds wrong to native ears.

#### Spelling rules for the -s form

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Most verbs: add **-s** | work → work**s**, run → run**s** |
| Verbs ending in -s, -sh, -ch, -x, -z, -o: add **-es** | watch → watch**es**, go → go**es**, do → do**es** |
| Consonant + y: change y to i + **-es** | study → stud**ies**, fly → fl**ies** |
| Vowel + y: just add **-s** | play → play**s**, enjoy → enjoy**s** |

#### Positive

```
subject + V1 (+ -s for he/she/it)
```

> I **work** in Jakarta.
> They **live** here.
> She **works** in Jakarta.
> The bus **arrives** at 7am.

#### Negative

```
subject + do not / don't (or does not / doesn't) + V1
```

| Subject | Auxiliary | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | do not / don't | I **don't work** on Sundays. |
| He / She / It | does not / doesn't | He **doesn't work** on Sundays. |

⚠️ After **doesn't / does not**, the verb returns to V1 — **no -s**.
- ❌ She **doesn't works** here.
- ✅ She **doesn't work** here.

> The -s is *already* on **does**. Don't put it on the verb too.

#### Question

```
Do / Does + subject + V1 ?
Wh-word + do / does + subject + V1 ?
```

> **Do** you **work** here?
> **Does** she **speak** Indonesian?
> **Where do** they **live**?
> **What does** he **want**?

⚠️ Same rule — after **does**, the verb is V1, not V1+s.

### "to be" in the present — am / is / are

The verb **to be** behaves differently. It does **not** use *do/does* — it makes its own forms directly.

| Subject | "to be" | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I | **am** | I **am** Indonesian. |
| You / We / They | **are** | They **are** students. |
| He / She / It | **is** | She **is** a doctor. |

#### Forms

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| Positive | I **am** tired. She **is** here. They **are** ready. |
| Negative | I **am not** tired. She **isn't** here. They **aren't** ready. |
| Question | **Am** I late? **Is** she here? **Are** they ready? |

⚠️ Never use *do/does* with "to be":
- ❌ **Does** she **is** Indonesian?
- ✅ **Is** she Indonesian?

> For the full picture across all tenses, see [Verb "to be" Master Guide →](/references/verb-to-be).

### Frequency adverbs — friends of the present simple

These adverbs almost always pair with the present simple. Memorize the **scale** and the **position**.

| Frequency | Adverb |
|---|---|
| 100% | always |
| ~90% | usually, normally, generally |
| ~70% | often, frequently |
| ~50% | sometimes |
| ~30% | occasionally |
| ~10% | rarely, seldom, hardly ever |
| 0% | never |

#### Position rule

```
Subject + [adverb] + main verb
Subject + "to be" + [adverb]
```

> I **always** drink coffee in the morning. *(before main verb)*
> She is **always** late. *(after "to be")*
> They **never** eat breakfast. *(before main verb)*
> He is **never** angry. *(after "to be")*

⚠️ Frequency adverbs go **between** the subject and the main verb — but **after** "to be".

### Common time markers

| Marker | Example |
|---|---|
| every day / week / month / year | She runs **every day**. |
| in the morning / afternoon / evening | I work **in the morning**. |
| at night | They study **at night**. |
| on Mondays / weekends | We meet **on Mondays**. |
| once / twice / three times a week | He calls **twice a week**. |

## Bahasa Indonesia

### The fundamental difference: subject-verb agreement

In Bahasa Indonesia, the verb **never changes for the subject**:

| Subject | Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|---|
| I | Saya **makan** | I **eat** |
| You | Kamu **makan** | You **eat** |
| He / She | Dia **makan** | He / She **eats** ⚠️ |
| We | Kami **makan** | We **eat** |
| They | Mereka **makan** | They **eat** |

The Indonesian verb *makan* is exactly the same five times. In English, **only one row gets the -s** — and that's the row Indonesian speakers forget.

This is the **#1 grammar mistake** Indonesian learners make in English. It feels invisible because the meaning is clear without it. But it's the easiest thing for a TOEFL/IELTS examiner to mark wrong.

### Why "do/does" feels redundant

In Bahasa Indonesia, you ask questions by:

1. Adding **apakah** at the beginning: *Apakah kamu makan?*
2. Or just using a question intonation: *Kamu makan?*

In English, you can't do either — you must add **do** or **does** as an
auxiliary. This feels like an extra word that does nothing useful.

| Indonesian | English |
|---|---|
| Apakah kamu **makan** nasi? | **Do** you **eat** rice? |
| Apakah dia **bekerja** di sini? | **Does** he **work** here? |
| Saya tidak **makan** daging. | I **don't eat** meat. |
| Dia tidak **bekerja** hari ini. | He **doesn't work** today. |

The "do/does" carries the **subject-verb agreement** information *and* signals
"this is a question or a negative." Without it, an English question or
negative collapses into a statement.

### "to be" is dropped in Indonesian

This will sound familiar from Simple Past — the same trap appears in present:

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Saya **lapar**. | I **am** hungry. |
| Dia **guru**. | She **is** a teacher. |
| Mereka **di rumah**. | They **are** at home. |
| **Dingin** sekali. | It **is** very cold. |

Indonesian doesn't need "am/is/are" — but English **always** does.

### Side-by-side: what changes, what doesn't

| Feature | Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|---|
| Verb changes by subject? | ❌ Never | ✅ Yes — adds -s for he/she/it |
| Auxiliary needed for questions? | No (just *apakah* or intonation) | Yes — do/does |
| Auxiliary needed for negatives? | No (*tidak* + verb) | Yes — don't/doesn't |
| "to be" required? | ❌ Often dropped | ✅ Always needed |
| Frequency adverb position | Flexible | Before main verb / after "to be" |

### Translation strategy

When translating an Indonesian sentence into English present simple:

1. **Find the subject.** Is it he / she / it / a singular thing? → verb gets **-s**.
2. **Is it negative?** → use *don't* or *doesn't* (matching the subject) + V1.
3. **Is it a question?** → start with *Do* or *Does* (matching the subject) + V1.
4. **Is the verb "to be"?** → use *am / is / are* directly. No do/does.
5. **Did Indonesian drop the verb?** → check if you need to **add** "to be" (am/is/are).

## Common Mistakes

These trace back to two root causes: **Bahasa Indonesia doesn't conjugate by
subject** and **doesn't use auxiliary verbs**.

**1. Forgetting the third-person -s**
- ❌ She **work** at the hospital.
- ✅ She **works** at the hospital.

  *Why it happens:* the subject doesn't change the verb in Indonesian. This is the most common mistake — train yourself to pause at he/she/it.

**2. Adding -s with the wrong subject**
- ❌ I **works** here.
- ❌ They **lives** in Bali.
- ✅ I **work** here. / They **live** in Bali.

  *Why it happens:* over-correction after learning the -s rule.

**3. Keeping the -s after does/doesn't**
- ❌ She **doesn't works** here.
- ❌ **Does** he **lives** here?
- ✅ She **doesn't work** here. / **Does** he **live** here?

  *Why it happens:* the "-s is for he/she/it" rule overpowers the "after does, V1" rule. Remember: the -s is already on *does*.

**4. Skipping do/does in questions or negatives**
- ❌ You **like** coffee? *(intonation only)*
- ❌ I **not eat** meat.
- ✅ **Do** you **like** coffee? / I **don't eat** meat.

  *Why it happens:* Indonesian uses *apakah* / intonation for questions and *tidak* + verb for negatives, with no auxiliary.

**5. Using do/does with "to be"**
- ❌ **Does** she **is** Indonesian?
- ❌ I **don't be** tired.
- ✅ **Is** she Indonesian? / I **am not** tired.

  *Why it happens:* over-applying the do/does pattern. "to be" makes its own forms.

**6. Dropping "to be"**
- ❌ I **happy** today.
- ❌ She **teacher**.
- ✅ I **am** happy today. / She **is** a teacher.

  *Why it happens:* Indonesian doesn't need "to be" — direct word-for-word translation drops it.

**7. Wrong frequency adverb placement**
- ❌ I drink **always** coffee.
- ❌ She always **is** tired. *(wait — this depends!)*
- ✅ I **always** drink coffee. *(before main verb)*
- ✅ She **is always** tired. *(after "to be")*

  *Why it happens:* Indonesian word order for *selalu, sering, kadang* is more flexible than English.

**8. Using present continuous instead of simple for routines**
- ❌ I **am working** at a hospital. *(when describing your job in general)*
- ✅ I **work** at a hospital.

  *Why it happens:* "am working" sounds more "active" so learners reach for it. But for a job or general state, present simple is right.
