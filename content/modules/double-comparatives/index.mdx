## Explanation

**Double comparatives** describe two things that change together proportionally. There are two main structures: the **the…the** structure (parallel change) and the **comparative + and + comparative** structure (gradual change).

### Structure 1: The…The (Parallel Change)

> **The + comparative, the + comparative**

This expresses that as one thing increases/decreases, another increases/decreases in proportion.

| First clause | Second clause |
|---|---|
| The more you practice, | the better you become. |
| The higher the temperature, | the faster the reaction. |
| The longer you wait, | the harder it will be. |
| The less sleep he gets, | the worse his concentration. |

**Rules for this structure:**
- Both clauses begin with **the**
- Both use a **comparative** form (not a base adjective)
- The first clause states the **condition**; the second states the **result**
- **No comma** is strictly required but is commonly used for readability
- A complete verb is optional in the second clause in informal speech: *The more, the merrier.*

### Academic Variations

In formal and academic writing:
- The more data researchers collect, **the more reliable** their conclusions become.
- The higher the investment, **the greater** the potential return.
- The less time available, **the more efficiently** teams tend to work.

### Structure 2: Comparative + and + Comparative (Gradual Change)

> **adjective-er and adjective-er** (short adjectives)
> **more and more + adjective** (long adjectives)

This expresses something that is continuously increasing or changing:

| Short adjective | Long adjective |
|---|---|
| It is getting **hotter and hotter**. | The problem is becoming **more and more complex**. |
| Prices are growing **bigger and bigger**. | The task seemed **more and more difficult**. |
| Traffic is becoming **worse and worse**. | She became **more and more confident**. |

### Less and Less (Decrease)

> **less and less + adjective/adverb**

- His enthusiasm became **less and less** visible over time.
- People are spending **less and less** time outdoors.
- The gaps in the data are becoming **less and less** significant.

### Common Error: Adding a Second Comparative in the Result Clause

> ❌ *The more you practice, the more you get better.*
> ✅ *The more you practice, the better you get.*

The result clause uses **only one comparative** — you cannot combine "more" with an -er/better form.

### Structure Summary

| Structure | Use |
|---|---|
| The + comparative, the + comparative | Proportional change between two variables |
| Comparative + and + comparative | Continuous/gradual change in one thing |
| Less and less | Continuous decrease |

## Bahasa Indonesia

### Perbedaan dengan Bahasa Indonesia

Bahasa Indonesia menggunakan "**semakin...semakin**" untuk menyatakan perubahan proporsional:
- *Semakin banyak kamu berlatih, semakin baik kemampuanmu.* → **The more** you practice, **the better** you become.
- *Harganya semakin mahal.* → Prices are getting **more and more expensive**.

### Tabel Kontras

| Bahasa Indonesia | ❌ Kesalahan umum | ✅ Inggris yang benar |
|---|---|---|
| Semakin banyak belajar, semakin pintar | The more study, the more smart | **The more you study, the smarter** you become |
| Semakin panas | More and more hot | **Hotter and hotter** |
| Semakin kompleks | Complexer and complexer | **More and more complex** |
| Semakin sedikit | More and more less | **Less and less** |

### Interferensi Khas

Pelajar Indonesia sering menerjemahkan "semakin...semakin" secara harfiah:
1. Menghilangkan "the" dari klausa pertama atau kedua
2. Menambahkan comparative ganda dalam hasil klausa: "the more you practise, the more you get better"
3. Menggunakan kata sifat dasar alih-alih comparative: "the more, the good" (salah) → "the more, the better" (benar)

## Common Mistakes

1. ❌ *The more you practice, the more you get better.* → ✅ *The more you practice, **the better** you get.* *Why:* The result clause needs only one comparative — don't combine "more" with "better."

2. ❌ *More and more, the problem becomes serious.* → ✅ *The problem is becoming **more and more serious**.* *Why:* "More and more" modifies the adjective directly; it is not a clause starter.

3. ❌ *The higher the temperature, the fast the reaction.* → ✅ *The higher the temperature, **the faster** the reaction.* *Why:* Both clauses must use the comparative — "fast" must be "faster."

4. ❌ *The more information we have, the better we can taking decisions.* → ✅ *The more information we have, the better decisions we can **take**.* *Why:* The verb form after the comparative must be grammatically correct.

5. ❌ *It is becoming more and more hot outside.* → ✅ *It is becoming **hotter and hotter** outside.* *Why:* "Hot" is a short one-syllable adjective — use -er and -er, not "more and more."

6. ❌ *Less and less people are reading newspapers.* → ✅ ***Fewer and fewer** people are reading newspapers.* *Why:* "People" is countable — use "fewer," not "less."

7. ❌ *More richer and richer.* → ✅ *Richer and richer.* *Why:* Don't double the comparative — use only -er or only "more + adjective."

8. ❌ *The more you sleep, better you feel.* → ✅ *The more you sleep, **the** better you feel.* *Why:* Both clauses need "the."

## TOEFL Practice

Double comparatives appear in TOEFL reading passages about trends (economic growth, climate, social change) and in writing tasks. Watch for:
- Correct use of **the…the** with two comparatives
- "more and more" vs comparative + and + comparative choice
- "fewer and fewer" (countable) vs "less and less" (uncountable)
- No extra comparative in the result clause

## Quick Summary

- **The + comparative, the + comparative** shows proportional change between two variables
- Both clauses need "the" and both need the comparative form — not the base adjective
- Result clause error: never add a second comparative — "the more you get better" is wrong
- **Comparative + and + comparative** shows continuous/gradual change in one thing
- Short adjectives: hotter and hotter, worse and worse
- Long adjectives: more and more complex, more and more difficult
- Decrease: **less and less** (uncountable/abstract); **fewer and fewer** (countable nouns)
- Indonesian "semakin...semakin" maps to "the...the" structure — but word order is stricter in English
