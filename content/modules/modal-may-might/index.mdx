## Explanation

**May** and **might** both express possibility. **May** suggests a slightly higher degree of certainty; **might** is more tentative or remote. Both are followed by V1. **May** is also used for formal permission.

### Possibility — may vs might

| Modal | Certainty | Example |
|---|---|---|
| may | ~50% possibility | It **may** rain this afternoon. |
| might | ~30% possibility (more tentative) | She **might** come — I'm not sure. |

> In practice, **may** and **might** are often interchangeable for possibility. The difference in certainty is subtle and context-dependent.

```
Subject + may / might + V1
```

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Future possibility | I **may** go to the conference next month. |
| Tentative possibility | He **might** know the answer — ask him. |
| Uncertain situation | The test results **may** take a few days. |
| Remote possibility | We **might** win, but the odds are against us. |

### Negative possibility — may not / might not

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| may not + V1 | It's possible that [subject] will not... |
| might not + V1 | It's possible (remotely) that [subject] will not... |

> She **may not** be available on Tuesday.
> He **might not** have heard the announcement.

**Important:** There is no contraction "mayn't" — always use "may not".

### Permission — formal use of "may"

"May" is used in formal contexts to ask for or give permission. It is more formal than "can":

| Formal | Less formal |
|---|---|
| **May** I use the bathroom? | **Can** I use the bathroom? |
| You **may** enter now. | You **can** enter now. |
| Guests **may not** use the pool after 10 pm. | Guests **can't** use the pool after 10 pm. |

> In academic and formal writing, use **may** (not "can") for permission.

### May / might vs could / can for possibility

| Modal | Use |
|---|---|
| can | General/theoretical possibility ("It **can** snow here in winter.") |
| could | Uncertain specific possibility ("She **could** be in the office.") |
| may | 50% possibility — a real option ("He **may** apply.") |
| might | Remote or tentative possibility ("I **might** go, but probably not.") |

### May / might for academic hedging

In academic writing, **may** and **might** are hedging devices that soften claims:

> The results **may** suggest a correlation between the two variables.
> This **might** indicate that further research is needed.
> The findings **may** not be generalisable to other populations.

### Summary table

| Function | May | Might |
|---|---|---|
| Present/future possibility | ✅ | ✅ |
| Negative possibility | may not | might not |
| Formal permission | ✅ | — |
| Academic hedging | ✅ | ✅ |
| Tentative/remote possibility | — | ✅ (more so) |

## Bahasa Indonesia

### "Mungkin" — the Indonesian equivalent

Indonesian uses **mungkin** (possibly) or **boleh** (may/permitted) for both possibility and permission.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Mungkin dia sedang rapat. | She **may** / **might** be in a meeting. |
| Mungkin saya tidak bisa hadir. | I **may not** / **might not** be able to attend. |
| Bolehkah saya masuk? | **May** I come in? (formal) |
| Kamu boleh pergi sekarang. | You **may** go now. (formal) |

### Common interference errors

**1. "Mungkin" + verb → "maybe" + modal (word order error)**

In Indonesian, *mungkin* comes before the verb. Indonesian learners sometimes use "maybe" + modal together:
- ❌ Maybe it might rain.
- ✅ It **may** rain. / It **might** rain. / **Maybe** it will rain.

"Maybe" is an adverb — you don't combine it with may/might in the same clause.

**2. Using "can" for formal permission (informal register)**

*Boleh saya…?* → formal English uses "may", not "can":
- Informal: Can I ask you something?
- Formal: **May** I ask you something?

**3. "Might not" vs "cannot" confusion**

*Mungkin tidak* = possibility of not → "might not" / "may not" (not "cannot"):
- ❌ She cannot attend tomorrow. *(certain inability)*
- ✅ She **might not** attend tomorrow. *(uncertain — possible she won't)*

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using "might" for certainty**
- ❌ She might definitely come — I just spoke to her. *(contradictory)*
- ✅ She **will** come — I just spoke to her.
- *Why:* "Might" implies uncertainty. Use "will" for certain future events.

**2. Using "maybe" + "might" together**
- ❌ Maybe she might call later.
- ✅ She **might** call later. / **Maybe** she'll call later.
- *Why:* "Maybe" and "might" both signal uncertainty. Don't double them up.

**3. Omitting "not" between "may" and the verb**
- ❌ She may comes tomorrow.
- ✅ She **may come** tomorrow.
- *Why:* Modal + V1 — always base form. No "-s" for third person after modals.

**4. "Mayn't" (non-standard contraction)**
- ❌ She mayn't be there.
- ✅ She **may not** be there.
- *Why:* "Mayn't" is rare and non-standard. Always write "may not" as two words.

**5. Using "might" for formal permission (too tentative)**
- ❌ Might I suggest an alternative? *(very rare, almost archaic)*
- ✅ **May** I suggest an alternative? *(formal, natural)*
- *Why:* "May" is the standard formal permission modal. "Might" for permission is archaic.

**6. Confusing "may not" (possibility) with "must not" (prohibition)**
- ❌ Visitors may not enter — it's dangerous! *(ambiguous — possibility or prohibition?)*
- ✅ Visitors **must not** enter — it's dangerous! *(prohibition)*
- *Why:* "May not" = it's possible they won't. "Must not" = they are forbidden. In formal signs, "may not" means prohibition, but in speech "must not" is clearer.

**7. Adding "to" after "may" or "might"**
- ❌ She might to call later.
- ✅ She **might** call later.
- *Why:* Modal verbs are always followed by V1 (base form) — no "to".

## Quick Summary

- **May** = ~50% possibility; also formal permission
- **Might** = ~30% possibility; more tentative or remote
- Both followed by V1 (base form) — never "may to go", "might comes"
- Negative: **may not** / **might not** — no "mayn't"
- Formal permission: "**May** I…?" is more formal than "Can I…?"
- Don't combine with "maybe": not "maybe it might rain" → "it **might** rain"
- Academic hedging: "results **may** suggest…", "this **might** indicate…"
- Distinguishing from "must": may/might = uncertain possibility; must = strong deduction or obligation
