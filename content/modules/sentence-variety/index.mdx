## Explanation

Academic writing at B2–C1 level requires **sentence variety** — mixing sentence lengths, types, and structures. Repeating the same subject-verb-object pattern makes prose monotonous and reduces the score on TOEFL/IELTS writing tasks.

The three main techniques are: **fronting**, **inversion**, and **cleft sentences (it-clefts and wh-clefts)**.

### Technique 1 — Fronting

**Fronting** moves an element that normally comes later in the sentence to the **front** for emphasis or cohesion. Unlike inversion, the subject and verb do **not** swap positions.

```
Normal:    She had never seen such a large crowd.
Fronted:   Such a large crowd she had never seen.   ← object moved front

Normal:    The results are presented in Table 1.
Fronted:   In Table 1, the results are presented.   ← adverbial moved front
```

Fronting adverbials (time, place, manner) is the most common and natural form:

> **In 2020,** the pandemic changed everything.
> **Quietly,** she closed the door.
> **At the top of the page,** the researcher wrote her name.

### Technique 2 — Subject-verb inversion

Inversion swaps the normal subject-verb order. It is required after certain negative or restrictive adverbials at the front of the sentence.

#### Negative adverbials — always trigger inversion

| Adverbial | Normal order | Inverted |
|---|---|---|
| Never | She had never seen this. | **Never had** she seen this. |
| Rarely / Seldom | He rarely missed a class. | **Rarely did** he miss a class. |
| Hardly / Scarcely | She had hardly finished when... | **Hardly had** she finished when... |
| Not only | Not only he worked hard... ❌ | **Not only did** he work hard... |
| No sooner | No sooner she arrived... ❌ | **No sooner had** she arrived... |
| Little | Little she knew... ❌ | **Little did** she know... |
| At no time | At no time he left... ❌ | **At no time did** he leave... |
| Under no circumstances | Under no circumstances you should... ❌ | **Under no circumstances should** you... |

#### Inversion pattern

```
Negative adverbial + auxiliary + subject + main verb
```

> **Not only did** she win the prize, but she also set a new record.
> **Rarely have** researchers observed such behaviour in the wild.
> **Hardly had** the ceremony begun **when** it started to rain.

⚠️ "No sooner...than" and "Hardly/Scarcely...when" are paired structures:
> No sooner **had** she sat down **than** the phone rang.
> Scarcely **had** he finished **when** the power went out.

#### "So" and "Neither/Nor" inversion

| Positive agreement | Negative agreement |
|---|---|
| A: I enjoy reading. B: **So do I.** | A: I don't like spicy food. B: **Neither do I.** |
| A: She has been to Paris. B: **So have I.** | A: He hasn't called. B: **Nor has she.** |

### Technique 3 — Cleft sentences

Cleft sentences split one idea into two clauses to **emphasize** one element. There are two types.

#### It-cleft

```
It + be + [emphasized element] + that/who + rest of sentence
```

> Normal: **Einstein** developed the theory of relativity.
> It-cleft: **It was Einstein** who developed the theory of relativity.

> Normal: She found the key **in the garden**.
> It-cleft: **It was in the garden** that she found the key.

> Normal: **The noise** woke the baby.
> It-cleft: **It was the noise** that woke the baby.

The element after "It was/is" receives the stress and emphasis.

#### Wh-cleft (pseudo-cleft)

```
What + clause + be + [emphasized element]
```

> Normal: We need more funding.
> Wh-cleft: **What we need** is more funding.

> Normal: She wants recognition.
> Wh-cleft: **What she wants** is recognition.

> Normal: He did the right thing.
> Wh-cleft: **What he did** was (the) right thing.

Wh-clefts are common in academic writing to introduce conclusions or findings.

### Varying sentence length

Short sentences create **impact**. Long sentences build **complexity**. A mix creates **rhythm**.

> The experiment failed. The cause was unclear. Further tests were needed. *(monotonous — all short)*

> The experiment failed; however, the cause remained unclear, and the research team determined that further tests would be required before any conclusions could be drawn. *(too long and unwieldy)*

> The experiment failed. The cause, however, remained unclear. Further tests were required. *(varied — effective)*

## Bahasa Indonesia

### Fronting is natural in Indonesian

Indonesian already uses fronting frequently — the word order is flexible. The challenge for English is knowing when inversion is **required** (after negative adverbials) vs. when it is optional (fronting adverbials).

| Bahasa Indonesia | English — fronting only | English — inversion required |
|---|---|---|
| Jarang dia terlambat. | Rarely, he is late. *(wrong)* | **Rarely is** he late. *(inversion required)* |
| Di Jakarta, dia bekerja. | **In Jakarta,** he works. *(fronting — no inversion)* | — |

### Cleft sentences have no direct Indonesian equivalent

Indonesian expresses emphasis through **stress, particles (yang, justru, memang),** and word order — not through cleft structures.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English equivalent |
|---|---|
| Yang membuat proyek ini berhasil adalah timnya. | **What made** this project succeed **was** the team. *(wh-cleft)* |
| Justru timnyalah yang berhasil. | **It was the team** that succeeded. *(it-cleft)* |

Indonesian learners often write flat, emphasis-free sentences in English. Cleft structures are the tool to fix this.

### "So do I" / "Neither do I" — unfamiliar structure

Indonesian expresses agreement with *saya juga* (me too) or *saya juga tidak* (me neither). The English inversion form is purely structural and has no Indonesian parallel:

| Bahasa Indonesia | Wrong English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| Saya suka kopi. Saya juga. | I like coffee. Me also. ❌ | I like coffee. **So do I.** ✅ |
| Dia tidak datang. Saya juga tidak. | She didn't come. Me neither. ❌ | She didn't come. **Neither did I.** ✅ |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Missing inversion after negative adverbials**
- ❌ Rarely he misses a deadline.
- ✅ Rarely **does** he miss a deadline.

**2. Wrong auxiliary in inversion**
- ❌ Never she has seen such a crowd.
- ✅ Never **has** she seen such a crowd.

**3. Wrong it-cleft structure — omitting "that/who"**
- ❌ It was the noise woke the baby.
- ✅ It was the noise **that** woke the baby.

**4. Using "what" incorrectly in wh-cleft**
- ❌ What we need it is more time.
- ✅ What we need **is** more time. *(no extra "it")*

**5. "No sooner" without "than"**
- ❌ No sooner had she arrived when the problems started.
- ✅ No sooner had she arrived **than** the problems started.

**6. "Hardly/Scarcely" with "than" instead of "when"**
- ❌ Hardly had they started than it rained.
- ✅ Hardly had they started **when** it rained.

**7. Fronting without comma**
- ❌ In the morning she drinks coffee.
- ✅ In the morning**,** she drinks coffee.

## Quick Summary

- **Fronting**: move an adverbial to the front + comma. Subject-verb order stays the same. *In 2020, the study began.*
- **Inversion**: negative adverbial at front → auxiliary moves before subject. *Never had she seen this.*
- Key negative adverbials that trigger inversion: **never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, not only, no sooner, little, at no time, under no circumstances**.
- **It-cleft**: *It was [emphasis] that/who [rest].* Emphasizes a specific element.
- **Wh-cleft**: *What [clause] is [emphasis].* Common in academic conclusions.
- Paired structures: **No sooner…than** | **Hardly/Scarcely…when**.
- Agreement inversions: **So do I** (positive) | **Neither/Nor do I** (negative).
