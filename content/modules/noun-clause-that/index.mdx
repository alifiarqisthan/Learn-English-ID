## Explanation

A **noun clause** functions as a noun inside a larger sentence. It can be a subject, object, or complement. There are three main types: **that-clauses**, **wh-clauses**, and **if/whether clauses**.

### Type 1 — That-clauses

A **that-clause** begins with *that* and functions as the object of a main verb, especially verbs of **thinking, believing, saying, knowing, and feeling**.

```
Main verb + that + subject + verb
```

> I know **that she is right**.
> She believes **that the project will succeed**.
> They announced **that the meeting was postponed**.

The word **"that" can often be omitted** in informal contexts when the clause is the object:
> I know *(that)* she is right. ✅
> She believes *(that)* he is wrong. ✅

However, **"that" cannot be omitted** when:
1. The clause is the **subject** of the sentence.
2. The clause follows a **noun** (noun + that): *the fact that*, *the idea that*, *the belief that*.
3. The clause follows **certain adjectives**: *It is important that…*, *It is clear that…*

> **That she won** the competition surprised everyone. *(subject — cannot omit "that")*
> The fact **that** she won surprised everyone. *(noun + that — cannot omit)*
> It is clear **that** more research is needed. *(adjective pattern)*

### Verbs commonly followed by that-clauses

| Category | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Thinking | think, believe, know, understand, realize, assume, suppose |
| Saying / reporting | say, report, announce, claim, argue, suggest, admit |
| Feeling | feel, hope, wish, fear, regret |
| Showing | show, prove, indicate, demonstrate, find |

> The study **shows that** exercise improves mental health.
> Scientists **believe that** the universe is expanding.
> She **admitted that** she had made a mistake.

### Type 2 — Wh-clauses (embedded questions)

A **wh-clause** begins with a wh-word (what, where, when, why, who, how, which) and functions as a noun. These are **embedded (indirect) questions** — the word order changes from question to statement form.

```
Question:   Where does she live?
Wh-clause:  I don't know where she lives.   (NOT: where does she live)
```

| Question | Wh-clause (embedded) |
|---|---|
| Where is she? | I wonder **where she is**. |
| What does he want? | I don't know **what he wants**. |
| Who called you? | Tell me **who called you**. |
| How did they solve it? | Nobody knows **how they solved it**. |
| Which option is better? | I can't decide **which option is better**. |

⚠️ **Critical rule:** In an embedded question, the word order is **statement order** (subject + verb), NOT question order (auxiliary + subject + verb).

- ❌ I don't know **where does she live**.
- ✅ I don't know **where she lives**.

- ❌ Can you tell me **what is the time**?
- ✅ Can you tell me **what the time is**?

### Type 3 — If/whether clauses

**If** and **whether** introduce embedded yes/no questions (questions with no wh-word).

```
Question:   Is the store open?
Embedded:   I don't know if / whether the store is open.
```

> Can you tell me **whether the meeting has been rescheduled**?
> She asked **if he had finished** the report.
> I'm not sure **whether to accept** the offer. *(whether + to-infinitive)*

**"Whether" vs. "if"**:

| Feature | Whether | If |
|---|---|---|
| Before to-infinitive | ✅ I don't know **whether to go**. | ❌ I don't know **if to go**. |
| As subject of sentence | ✅ **Whether she agrees** matters. | ❌ **If she agrees** matters. |
| After prepositions | ✅ The question of **whether** it works. | ❌ The question of **if** it works. |
| Object of verb | ✅ Both correct | ✅ Both correct |

### The "it" subject pattern

When a that-clause is the subject, English often uses a **dummy "it"** instead of putting the clause first:

```
That she won surprised everyone.   ← grammatical but formal/heavy
It surprised everyone that she won.  ← more natural
```

Common patterns:
> **It is clear that** more research is needed.
> **It is important that** students attend all classes.
> **It is surprising that** nobody noticed.
> **It seems that** the problem is more complex than expected.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### "Bahwa" — the Indonesian that-clause connector

Indonesian uses **bahwa** to introduce that-clauses in formal writing, or simply places the clause after the verb in informal speech.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Saya tahu bahwa dia benar. | I know **that** she is right. |
| Penelitian menunjukkan bahwa olahraga bermanfaat. | Research shows **that** exercise is beneficial. |
| Dia berkata bahwa dia akan datang. | She said **that** she would come. |

In informal Indonesian, *bahwa* is often dropped, just as "that" can be dropped in English:
> *Saya tahu dia benar.* = I know *(that)* she is right.

### Embedded questions — the biggest word order trap

This is the **most tested TOEFL structure** for Indonesian learners. In Indonesian, embedded questions keep the **same word order as the direct question**:

| Bahasa Indonesia | Direct word order (wrong in English) | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| Saya tidak tahu di mana dia tinggal. | I don't know **where does she live**. ❌ | I don't know **where she lives**. ✅ |
| Tolong katakan kepada saya apa waktu sekarang. | Please tell me **what is the time**. ❌ | Please tell me **what the time is**. ✅ |
| Saya tidak yakin apakah toko itu buka. | I'm not sure **is the store open**. ❌ | I'm not sure **if/whether the store is open**. ✅ |

The Indonesian pattern carries directly into English as a word order error. This is a major source of TOEFL Writing/Speaking errors.

### Translation checklist for embedded questions

When you see a question embedded in a sentence:
1. Remove the auxiliary/question inversion.
2. Keep: wh-word + subject + verb (statement order).
3. For yes/no questions: use *if* or *whether* + statement order.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Question word order inside an embedded clause**
- ❌ I don't know **what does he want**.
- ✅ I don't know **what he wants**.

**2. Using "if" before a to-infinitive**
- ❌ I don't know **if to accept** the offer.
- ✅ I don't know **whether to accept** the offer.

**3. Omitting "that" when the clause is the subject**
- ❌ She passed the exam surprised everyone.
- ✅ **That** she passed the exam surprised everyone.
- ✅ **It** surprised everyone **that** she passed the exam. *(more natural)*

**4. Adding "do/does" inside a wh-clause**
- ❌ Tell me where **does** she work.
- ✅ Tell me where she works.

**5. Forgetting "it is" in the dummy-subject pattern**
- ❌ Is clear that more research is needed.
- ✅ **It is** clear that more research is needed.

**6. Using "that" after "whether"**
- ❌ Whether **that** she will come is unclear.
- ✅ Whether she will come is unclear.

**7. "Say" vs. "tell" — object requirement**
- ❌ She **said me** that she was tired.
- ✅ She **told me** that she was tired.
- ✅ She **said** (to me) that she was tired.

  *"Say" does not take a direct person object — "tell" requires one.*

## Quick Summary

- **That-clause**: main verb + **that** + subject + verb. Expresses thoughts, beliefs, statements. "That" can be omitted in objects but NOT as subject or after nouns.
- **Wh-clause**: wh-word + **statement order** (subject + verb). ❌ "where does she live" → ✅ "where she lives".
- **If/whether clause**: embeds a yes/no question. "Whether" is required before to-infinitive, as subject, and after prepositions.
- **Dummy "it"**: *It is clear that / It is important that / It seems that* — avoids fronting the clause.
- **"Tell"** requires a person object; **"say"** does not.
- Common verbs: know, believe, think, show, find, argue, suggest, admit, realize, announce.
