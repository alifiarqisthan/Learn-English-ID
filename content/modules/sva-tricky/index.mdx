## Explanation

Beyond basic singular/plural agreement, several patterns regularly trip up advanced learners. This module covers the three most tested tricky cases: **intervening phrases**, **collective nouns**, and **nouns with misleading forms**.

### Tricky Case 1 — Intervening phrases

An **intervening phrase** is a phrase that comes between the subject and the verb. It can make the wrong noun seem like the subject. The rule: **ignore the intervening phrase** and find the true subject.

Common intervening phrases:
- *together with, along with, as well as, in addition to*
- *including, such as*
- *of + noun* (prepositional phrases)
- relative clauses (*who, which, that*)
- participial phrases (*working, based, located*)

#### Prepositional phrases (of + noun)

> The **box** of chocolates **was** opened. *(box = subject, chocolates = intervening)*
> The **quality** of the results **depends** on the method. *(quality = subject)*
> A **list** of items **is** attached. *(list = subject)*

The noun after "of" never determines the verb — find the head noun.

#### "Together with / along with / as well as"

These phrases **do not** create compound subjects. The verb still agrees with the **original subject**:

> The **director**, along with the board members, **was** present. *(director = singular)*
> The **manager**, together with her team, **is** responsible. *(manager = singular)*

Compare: "The director **and** the board members **were** present." *(and = compound subject → plural)*

#### "Including / such as"

> **She**, including all her assistants, **was** responsible for the project.
> The **findings**, such as temperature data, **indicate** a clear trend.

Still singular — the intervening phrase doesn't change the subject.

### Tricky Case 2 — Collective nouns

**Collective nouns** refer to a group of people or things using a singular form. The verb choice depends on whether you view the group as a **unit** or as **individuals**.

| Collective noun | Seen as a unit (singular) | Seen as individuals (plural) |
|---|---|---|
| team | The team **is** ready. | The team **are** arguing among themselves. |
| committee | The committee **has** reached a decision. | The committee **are** divided on the issue. |
| government | The government **is** considering new laws. | The government **are** disagreeing on the approach. |
| jury | The jury **has** returned a verdict. | The jury **are** still deliberating. |
| family | The family **is** moving to Jakarta. | The family **are** all in different cities. |
| class | The class **was** cancelled. | The class **were** talking among themselves. |
| staff | The staff **is** well-trained. | The staff **were** asking various questions. |

**British English** tends to use plural verbs with collective nouns more often.
**American English** tends to use singular verbs.

> For TOEFL (American English standard): treat collective nouns as **singular** unless context clearly shows individual action.

#### Other collective-like nouns (always singular)

> **The news** **is** shocking. *(looks plural — always singular)*
> **Mathematics** **is** a required subject. *(academic disciplines — singular)*
> **Physics / economics / politics** **is** a challenging field.
> **The United States** **is** a large country. *(proper nouns ending in -s)*

### Tricky Case 3 — Nouns with misleading forms

Some nouns look plural but are singular; some look singular but are plural.

#### Looks plural — actually singular

| Noun | Agreement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| news | singular | The **news is** alarming. |
| mathematics / physics / economics | singular | **Mathematics is** my favourite subject. |
| The United States / Philippines | singular (country) | **The Philippines is** an archipelago. |
| means (= method) | singular or plural | A **means** of transport **is** needed. / Several **means are** available. |
| series | singular or plural | The **series is** cancelled. / Three **series were** produced. |
| species | singular or plural | This **species is** endangered. / Several **species are** at risk. |

#### Always plural (no singular form)

> **The police are** investigating. *(never "the police is")*
> **The staff are** ready. *(often plural in British English)*
> **Scissors are** sharp. / I need a pair of scissors. *(plural noun)*
> **Trousers are** on the table. / A pair of trousers **is** on the table.
> **The elderly are** vulnerable. / **The rich are** not always happy.

#### "The + adjective" referring to a group — always plural

> **The poor are** in need of support.
> **The injured were** taken to hospital.
> **The unemployed are** seeking new opportunities.

### Tricky Case 4 — Relative clauses

When a relative clause modifies the subject, the verb in the **main clause** still agrees with the **main subject**, not the noun in the relative clause:

> **The student** who won all the prizes **is** from our department. *(student = singular)*
> **The students** who failed the test **need** extra support. *(students = plural)*
> **The report** that was submitted by all the researchers **is** ready. *(report = singular)*

The relative clause (in bold) is not the subject — it only modifies it.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### "Berita" — a seemingly countable noun

Indonesian *berita* (news) can often be used with numerals in informal speech. In English, **news** is always uncountable and always singular:
- ❌ The news **are** shocking.
- ✅ The news **is** shocking.

### Collective nouns — Indonesian uses plural freely

In Indonesian, group words like *tim, komite, pemerintah* can take plural markers or plural verbs naturally. In English, the singular/unit approach is preferred (especially for TOEFL):

| Bahasa Indonesia | English (preferred for TOEFL) |
|---|---|
| Tim itu sedang berdebat. | The team **is** debating. *(unit view)* |
| Pemerintah mempertimbangkan kebijakan baru. | The government **is** considering a new policy. |

### Intervening phrases — "beserta" / "bersama dengan"

*Beserta* / *bersama dengan* (along with, together with) in Indonesian may feel like they add to the subject. In English, they do not:

| Bahasa Indonesia | Wrong | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Direktur beserta anggota dewan hadir. | The director along with the board members **were** present. ❌ | The director, along with the board members, **was** present. ✅ |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Verb agrees with the intervening noun instead of the subject**
- ❌ The quality of the results **depend** on the method.
- ✅ The quality of the results **depends** on the method. *(quality = singular subject)*

**2. "Along with/together with" treated as "and"**
- ❌ The teacher, along with her students, **are** presenting.
- ✅ The teacher, along with her students, **is** presenting.

**3. "News" treated as plural**
- ❌ The news **are** good today.
- ✅ The news **is** good today.

**4. Academic subjects treated as plural**
- ❌ Economics **are** a difficult subject.
- ✅ Economics **is** a difficult subject.

**5. "The Philippines / The United States" with plural verb**
- ❌ The Philippines **are** an archipelago.
- ✅ The Philippines **is** an archipelago.

**6. "The + adjective group" with singular verb**
- ❌ The elderly **is** often overlooked.
- ✅ The elderly **are** often overlooked.

**7. "The police" with singular verb**
- ❌ The police **is** investigating the case.
- ✅ The police **are** investigating the case.

**8. Collective noun with wrong verb in TOEFL context**
- ❌ The committee **are** ready. *(for TOEFL — American English)*
- ✅ The committee **is** ready. *(American English default — unit view)*

## Quick Summary

- **Intervening phrases** (of, along with, together with, as well as) do NOT change the subject. Find the head noun.
- **"A box of chocolates"** → box = subject → singular verb.
- **"Along with"** ≠ "and" — doesn't create a compound subject.
- **Collective nouns** (team, committee, government, jury): TOEFL → singular (unit view).
- **"News, mathematics, physics, economics"** → always singular.
- **"The United States, The Philippines"** → singular (country).
- **"The police, the elderly, the poor"** → always plural.
- **"The + adjective group"** (the injured, the unemployed) → always plural.
- Verb in main clause agrees with **main subject**, not the noun in a relative clause.
