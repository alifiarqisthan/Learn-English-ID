## Explanation

**Modal perfects** combine a modal verb with **have + V3** to reason about past events. Instead of stating facts, you interpret evidence and draw conclusions about what probably happened.

### Structure

```
Subject + modal + have + V3
```

| Modal + have + V3 | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must have + V3 | Strong positive deduction — almost certain | She **must have left** early. |
| can't have + V3 | Strong negative deduction — impossible | He **can't have known** — he wasn't there. |
| could have + V3 | Past possibility (it was possible) | They **could have taken** a different route. |
| may have + V3 | 50% past possibility | She **may have already sent** it. |
| might have + V3 | Tentative/remote past possibility | He **might have forgotten**. |

### Must have + V3 — strong positive deduction

When you are almost certain something happened, based on evidence:

> The lights are off and the door is locked. They **must have gone** home.
> She got 100% — she **must have studied** very hard.
> You look exhausted. You **must have worked** all night.

### Can't have + V3 — strong negative deduction

When you are almost certain something did NOT happen:

> He **can't have seen** the announcement — he would have called us.
> The key is still on the hook. She **can't have left** yet.
> They arrived on time, so they **can't have missed** the train.

### Could have / may have / might have — past possibility

These express varying degrees of past possibility:

| Structure | Certainty | Example |
|---|---|---|
| could have + V3 | Possible (neutral) | She **could have gone** to the library. |
| may have + V3 | ~50% possibility | He **may have misunderstood** the question. |
| might have + V3 | ~30% (more tentative) | They **might have left** already. |

> "Could have" also expresses an **unrealised possibility** — something that was possible but didn't happen. See modal-perfect-regret for more.

### Comparing modals on the certainty scale

```
← Less certain ————————————————————— More certain →
might have    may have    could have    must have
           |                                    |
     (tentative)                       (near certain)
                                      can't have (near certain NEGATIVE)
```

### Common deduction contexts

| Situation | Deduction |
|---|---|
| No answer at the door | She **must have gone** out. |
| File is missing | Someone **must have deleted** it. |
| He looks pale | He **might have been** ill. |
| First in class | She **must have prepared** thoroughly. |
| Arrived late | He **may have missed** the bus. |

## Bahasa Indonesia

### "Pasti sudah" / "Mungkin sudah"

Indonesian uses **pasti sudah** (must have), **tidak mungkin sudah** (can't have), **mungkin sudah** (may/might have):

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Dia pasti sudah pergi. | She **must have left**. |
| Dia tidak mungkin melihatnya. | He **can't have seen** it. |
| Mungkin dia sudah tahu. | She **may have known** / **might have known**. |
| Bisa saja dia salah jalan. | He **could have taken** the wrong turn. |

### Why Indonesian learners struggle

**1. "Pasti" → always "must"**: Students use "must" for both present and past deduction without adding "have":
- ❌ She must left early. *(missing "have")*
- ✅ She **must have left** early.

**2. Word order — "have" placement**

The "have" must come immediately after the modal:
- ❌ She must left have early.
- ✅ She must **have left** early.

**3. V3 form**

The verb after "have" must be V3 (past participle), not V2 (simple past):
- ❌ He can't have went there.
- ✅ He **can't have gone** there.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Omitting "have" in modal perfect**
- ❌ She must left already — the lights are off.
- ✅ She **must have left** already.
- *Why:* Modal + have + V3 is the fixed structure. "Have" cannot be dropped.

**2. Using V2 instead of V3 after "have"**
- ❌ He can't have went to the meeting.
- ✅ He **can't have gone** to the meeting.
- *Why:* "Have" is always followed by the past participle (V3), not simple past (V2).

**3. Confusing "must have" (deduction) with "had to" (obligation)**
- ❌ She must have submit the form before 5 pm. *(mixing deduction + obligation)*
- ✅ She **must have submitted** the form — it's in the system. *(deduction)*
- ✅ She **had to** submit the form before 5 pm. *(obligation)*
- *Why:* "Must have + V3" is always deduction about the past, not past obligation.

**4. "Couldn't have" vs "can't have"**
- "Can't have + V3" = standard form for negative past deduction
- "Couldn't have + V3" = also correct, but usually implies a stricter impossibility or is used in conditional contexts
- Both are acceptable but "can't have" is more common for deduction.

**5. Using "mustn't have" for negative deduction**
- ❌ He mustn't have known — he looked surprised.
- ✅ He **can't have known** — he looked surprised.
- *Why:* "Mustn't" = prohibition (present). "Can't have" = negative past deduction. Never use "mustn't have" for deductions.

**6. Confusing "may have" (past deduction) with "may" (present possibility)**
- ❌ She may finished already. *(mixing tenses)*
- ✅ She **may have finished** already.
- *Why:* Past possibility/deduction always needs "have + V3" after the modal.

## Quick Summary

- **Must have + V3** = strong positive deduction: "She **must have known**."
- **Can't have + V3** = strong negative deduction: "He **can't have seen** it."
- **Could have / may have / might have + V3** = varying degrees of past possibility
- Structure is fixed: modal + **have** + **V3** — never drop "have", never use V2
- Scale: might have (least certain) → may have → could have → must have (most certain)
- "Mustn't have" = NOT used for deduction — use "can't have" for negative
- Indonesian *pasti sudah* → must have; *tidak mungkin* → can't have; *mungkin sudah* → may/might have
