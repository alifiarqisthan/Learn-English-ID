## Explanation

The **present perfect** is the tense that links the past to the present. It says:
"something happened in the past — and it still matters now."

> "I **have lost** my keys." — it happened in the past, and the result is relevant now (I still can't find them).
> "She **has lived** in Jakarta for 5 years." — it started in the past and is still true.
> "He **has visited** Bali three times." — the experience accumulated up to now.

This tense is **not about when** exactly something happened. The moment stays
unspecified. The moment you give a specific past time, you must switch to
simple past.

> See [Sentence Structure Formulas →](/references/sentence-structure) for the full comparison table across tenses.

### When to use it

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Experience (ever/never) | I **have eaten** natto before. Have you **ever visited** Europe? |
| Result still relevant now | She **has broken** her leg. (she can't walk now) |
| Unfinished time period | I **have studied** a lot **this week**. (the week isn't over) |
| Duration up to now (for/since) | They **have worked** here **for** 10 years / **since** 2014. |
| News / recent events (just/already/yet) | The prime minister **has just resigned**. |
| Talking about life up to now | I **have never failed** an exam. |

### V1, V2, V3 — which form?

The present perfect uses **V3 (past participle)**, not V2.

| Form | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | Base | write |
| V2 | Simple past | wrote |
| **V3** | **Past participle** | **written** |

> For regular verbs, V3 = V2 = V1 + **-ed** (worked, studied, lived).
> For irregular verbs, V3 is often different from V2 and must be memorized.
> See [Verb Forms Guide →](/references/verb-forms) and [Top 100 Irregular Verbs →](/references/irregular-verbs).

### Form — have/has + V3

#### Positive

```
subject + have/has + V3
```

| Subject | Auxiliary | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | **have** | I **have finished** the report. |
| He / She / It | **has** | She **has finished** the report. |

> "Has" only for he/she/it — the same rule as the third-person -s.

#### Negative

```
subject + have/has + not + V3
→ haven't / hasn't + V3
```

> I **haven't eaten** breakfast yet.
> She **hasn't called** me back.
> They **have not replied** to the email.

#### Question

```
Have/Has + subject + V3 ?
Wh-word + have/has + subject + V3 ?
```

> **Have** you **seen** this movie before?
> **Has** she **finished** the exam?
> **How long have** they **known** each other?

### Key time markers

These are the signal words that tell you present perfect is needed:

| Marker | Notes |
|---|---|
| **for** | duration of time: *for two hours, for years* |
| **since** | starting point: *since Monday, since 2019, since I was young* |
| **ever** | "at any time up to now" — in questions: *Have you ever...?* |
| **never** | "at no time up to now" |
| **already** | sooner than expected — in statements: *I've already eaten* |
| **yet** | in negatives + questions: *not yet, have you finished yet?* |
| **just** | very recently: *I've just arrived* |
| **recently / lately / so far** | general "up to now" frame |
| **this week/month/year** | the period is unfinished |
| **today / this morning** | unfinished time period |

⚠️ If you can answer "when exactly?" with a specific past time, **simple past** is needed — not present perfect.

### Present Perfect vs Simple Past — the key split

This is the hardest distinction for Indonesian learners. The rule is one sentence:

> **Specific past time → Simple Past. No specific time → Present Perfect.**

| Sentence | Tense | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I **ate** rice **yesterday**. | Simple Past | "yesterday" = specific past time |
| I **have eaten** rice before. | Present Perfect | no specific time, talking about experience |
| She **called** me **at 3pm**. | Simple Past | "at 3pm" = specific |
| She **has called** me twice **today**. | Present Perfect | "today" = unfinished period |
| He **lived** in Bali **from 2010 to 2015**. | Simple Past | "from…to" = closed period |
| He **has lived** in Bali **since 2015**. | Present Perfect | "since" = open period up to now |

⚠️ TOEFL/IELTS specifically tests this split in Error Identification and Sentence Completion.

### "For" vs "Since"

Both are used with present perfect for duration but they answer different questions:

| Word | Answer to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **for** | "how long?" (a span of time) | for 3 days, for years, for a long time |
| **since** | "when did it start?" (a point in time) | since Monday, since 2015, since she was born |

> ❌ I have lived here **since 5 years**.
> ✅ I have lived here **for 5 years**.
> ✅ I have lived here **since 2019**.

## Bahasa Indonesia

### The "sudah / telah" trap

In Bahasa Indonesia, **sudah** and **telah** are the words that feel most like
present perfect. But they don't map cleanly — which causes two opposite errors.

**Error 1: Using simple past when present perfect is needed**

Indonesian learners often use simple past everywhere because Bahasa Indonesia
tends to express *sudah* contexts with simple past tense in English study.

| Indonesian | Wrong English | Right English |
|---|---|---|
| Saya sudah makan. | I **ate**. | I **have eaten**. |
| Dia sudah pergi. | She **went**. | She **has gone** / She **has left**. |

**Error 2: Using present perfect when simple past is needed**

Conversely, when learners *know* about present perfect, they sometimes
over-apply it — especially with *sudah* + past time.

| Indonesian | Wrong English | Right English |
|---|---|---|
| Saya sudah makan **kemarin**. | I **have eaten** yesterday. | I **ate** yesterday. |
| Dia sudah pergi **tadi malam**. | She **has left** last night. | She **left** last night. |

**The fix:** check for a specific past time word. If it's there → simple past. If
not → present perfect.

### Why "have/has" feels redundant

In Bahasa Indonesia there is no auxiliary verb equivalent to *have/has* in
this tense. The sentence *"Saya sudah makan"* has only one verb: *makan*.
English requires **two words** — the auxiliary *have/has* + V3.

This causes learners to drop *have/has* or use it without V3:

- ❌ I **finished** already. *(present perfect idea but wrong form)*
- ❌ I **have finish** already. *(missing V3)*
- ✅ I **have finished** already.

### Side-by-side: present perfect vs simple past in Indonesian

| Situation | Indonesian | English |
|---|---|---|
| Specific finished past | Saya makan nasi **kemarin**. | I **ate** rice yesterday. |
| Unspecified experience | Saya pernah makan sushi. | I **have eaten** sushi. |
| Duration to now | Saya **sudah** belajar **selama** 2 jam. | I **have studied** for 2 hours. |
| Just happened | Dia **baru saja** tiba. | She **has just arrived**. |
| Not done yet | Saya **belum** makan. | I **haven't eaten** yet. |

Note how Indonesian *sudah, pernah, belum, baru saja* all map to different
present perfect constructions in English.

### The "ever/never/pernah" connection

Indonesian *pernah* = "have ever" and *belum pernah* = "have never." These
map directly to present perfect:

| Indonesian | English |
|---|---|
| Saya **pernah** ke Jepang. | I **have been** to Japan. |
| Saya **belum pernah** naik pesawat. | I **have never** flown. |
| Apakah kamu **pernah** makan durian? | **Have** you **ever eaten** durian? |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using simple past with unfinished time periods**
- ❌ I **studied** a lot this week.
- ✅ I **have studied** a lot this week.

  *Why it happens:* "this week" feels like a time marker → learner uses simple past. But the week isn't over — the period is unfinished → present perfect.

**2. Using present perfect with specific past times**
- ❌ I **have eaten** lunch **at noon**.
- ❌ She **has called** me **yesterday**.
- ✅ I **ate** lunch at noon. / She **called** me yesterday.

  *Why it happens:* *sudah* is translated as present perfect regardless of whether a specific time is given.

**3. Using V2 instead of V3 after have/has**
- ❌ I **have went** to the store.
- ❌ She **has wrote** the letter.
- ✅ I **have gone** to the store.
- ✅ She **has written** the letter.

  *Why it happens:* learners remember the V2 form but use it here. Present perfect needs V3.

**4. Using "have" for he/she/it**
- ❌ He **have** finished already.
- ❌ She **have** lived here for 5 years.
- ✅ He **has** finished. / She **has** lived here.

  *Why it happens:* no subject-verb distinction in Bahasa Indonesia — *sudah* doesn't change form.

**5. Confusing "for" and "since"**
- ❌ I have lived here **since** 3 years.
- ❌ She has worked here **for** 2015.
- ✅ I have lived here **for** 3 years. (span)
- ✅ She has worked here **since** 2015. (point in time)

**6. Dropping "have/has"**
- ❌ I **already finished** the homework. *(technically simple past, wrong meaning)*
- ✅ I **have already finished** the homework.

  *Why it happens:* *sudah* translates directly as *already* — learners forget the auxiliary.

**7. Using "been to" vs "gone to"**
- ❌ She has **gone** to Paris. *(she's still there — hasn't come back)*
- ✅ She has **been** to Paris. *(she went and came back — experience)*
- ✅ She has **gone** to Paris. *(she's there right now)*

## Quick Summary

- Present perfect = **have/has + V3**. V3, not V2.
- Use when: experience (ever/never), result still relevant, duration up to now (for/since), unfinished time period, recent events (just/already/yet).
- **Specific past time → Simple Past. No specific time → Present Perfect.**
- *sudah/telah* + specific time → Simple Past (e.g. "I ate yesterday").
- *sudah/telah* + no time → Present Perfect (e.g. "I have eaten").
- **for** = span of time ("for 3 years"); **since** = starting point ("since 2019").
- **has** only for he/she/it; **have** for everything else.
- "have been to" = visited and came back; "have gone to" = went and is still there.
