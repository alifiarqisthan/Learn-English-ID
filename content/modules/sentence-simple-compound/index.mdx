## Explanation

A **simple sentence** has one independent clause: one subject and one predicate. It expresses a complete thought on its own.

> She studies every night.
> The professor cancelled the lecture.
> Rain and wind damaged the crops.

Note: a simple sentence can have a compound subject or compound verb — as long as it has only **one independent clause**.

> **Rain and wind** damaged the crops. *(compound subject — still simple)*
> She **studies and reads** every night. *(compound verb — still simple)*

### Fragments — incomplete sentences

A **fragment** is a group of words that lacks a subject, a verb, or a complete thought. Fragments are a common error in academic writing.

| Fragment | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Because she was tired. | Dependent clause, no main clause | She left early **because she was tired**. |
| Running down the hall. | Participial phrase, no subject/verb | **The student was** running down the hall. |
| A very interesting result. | Noun phrase, no verb | **It was** a very interesting result. |

### Run-on sentences

A **run-on** joins two independent clauses without proper punctuation or a conjunction.

| Run-on | Fix |
|---|---|
| She studied hard she passed the exam. | She studied hard**; she passed** the exam. |
| He was tired he kept working. | He was tired**, but** he kept working. |

### Compound sentences

A **compound sentence** joins two or more independent clauses using a **coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS) or a **semicolon**.

```
Independent clause + , + coordinating conjunction + independent clause
Independent clause + ; + independent clause
```

#### FANBOYS — the seven coordinating conjunctions

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **F**or | reason/cause (formal) | She left early, **for** she had a meeting. |
| **A**nd | addition | He studied, **and** she read. |
| **N**or | negative addition | She didn't call, **nor** did she write. |
| **B**ut | contrast | I wanted coffee, **but** they only had tea. |
| **O**r | alternative | You can study now, **or** you can fail later. |
| **Y**et | contrast (formal) | It was difficult, **yet** he succeeded. |
| **S**o | result | It rained, **so** we stayed inside. |

> Always put a **comma before the conjunction** when joining two full independent clauses.
> - ❌ She studied hard but she failed.
> - ✅ She studied hard, **but** she failed.

#### Semicolon

A semicolon joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction.

> She studied hard**;** she passed.
> The results were clear**;** the experiment was a success.

#### Conjunctive adverbs

After a semicolon, you can add a **conjunctive adverb** (however, therefore, moreover, consequently, nevertheless, furthermore) followed by a comma.

```
Independent clause ; conjunctive adverb , independent clause
```

> She studied hard**; however,** she still failed.
> The data was incomplete**; therefore,** the results were inconclusive.

⚠️ A conjunctive adverb is **not** a coordinating conjunction — you cannot use it with just a comma.
- ❌ She studied hard, **however** she failed.
- ✅ She studied hard**; however,** she failed.

### Summary of sentence types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | 1 independent clause | She studies. |
| Compound | 2+ independent clauses + conjunction/semicolon | She studies, and he reads. |

## Bahasa Indonesia

### No "fanboys" system in Indonesian

Bahasa Indonesia uses **dan** (and), **tetapi/tapi** (but), **atau** (or), **jadi/maka** (so) — but there is no strict rule about commas before conjunctions. The comma rule in English is more rigid.

| Bahasa Indonesia | English |
|---|---|
| Dia belajar dan dia lulus. | She studied, **and** she passed. *(comma before "and")* |
| Saya lelah tapi saya tetap bekerja. | I was tired, **but** I kept working. *(comma before "but")* |
| Hujan turun jadi kami di dalam. | It rained, **so** we stayed inside. *(comma before "so")* |

### Fragments are easy to produce from Indonesian

Indonesian sentences often omit the subject or verb in context. In English, both are always required.

| Bahasa Indonesia | Problem in English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| Karena hujan. | Fragment — no main clause | **We stayed home** because it rained. |
| Sangat menarik. | Fragment — no subject or verb | **It is** very interesting. |
| Pergi ke kampus. | Fragment — no subject | **He went** to campus. |

### Run-ons from direct translation

Indonesian writers often join sentences with commas or nothing at all, because the pacing feels natural. In English this creates run-ons.

| Bahasa Indonesia | Direct (wrong) | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Dia lelah, dia tidur siang. | He was tired, he took a nap. ❌ | He was tired**, so** he took a nap. |
| Saya suka kopi saya tidak suka teh. | I like coffee I don't like tea. ❌ | I like coffee**, but** I don't like tea. |

### Conjunctive adverbs vs. Indonesian transition words

Indonesian *namun*, *oleh karena itu*, *selain itu* map to English *however*, *therefore*, *furthermore* — but the punctuation rule is different.

| Bahasa Indonesia | Wrong English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| Dia belajar, namun dia gagal. | She studied, however she failed. ❌ | She studied**; however,** she failed. ✅ |
| Datanya tidak lengkap, oleh karena itu hasilnya tidak valid. | The data was incomplete, therefore... ❌ | The data was incomplete**; therefore,...** ✅ |

## Common Mistakes

**1. Missing comma before coordinating conjunction**
- ❌ She studied hard but she failed the exam.
- ✅ She studied hard, **but** she failed the exam.

  *Why:* comma is required before a coordinating conjunction that joins two full independent clauses.

**2. Comma splice — joining two clauses with only a comma**
- ❌ She studied hard, she passed the exam.
- ✅ She studied hard, **and** she passed. / She studied hard**;** she passed.

  *Why:* a comma alone cannot join two independent clauses.

**3. Using a conjunctive adverb after a comma (not a semicolon)**
- ❌ She was tired, however she finished the report.
- ✅ She was tired**; however,** she finished the report.

  *Why:* conjunctive adverbs are not coordinating conjunctions — they need a semicolon before them.

**4. Writing fragments**
- ❌ Because the exam was difficult.
- ✅ She failed **because the exam was difficult**.

  *Why:* "because" introduces a dependent clause — it cannot stand alone.

**5. Run-on from direct Indonesian translation**
- ❌ I was hungry I ate lunch early.
- ✅ I was hungry**, so** I ate lunch early.

**6. Adding "nor" without subject-verb inversion**
- ❌ She didn't call, nor she wrote.
- ✅ She didn't call, nor **did she** write.

  *Why:* after *nor*, the subject and auxiliary invert.

## Quick Summary

- A **simple sentence** = one independent clause (subject + predicate).
- A **compound sentence** = two independent clauses joined by a **FANBOYS conjunction + comma** or a **semicolon**.
- FANBOYS: **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so** — comma goes *before* the conjunction.
- A **semicolon** alone joins two clauses; a conjunctive adverb (however, therefore...) follows the semicolon and needs its own comma after it.
- **Fragment** = missing subject, verb, or complete thought — always fix these.
- **Run-on** = two independent clauses joined with no punctuation or just a comma — always fix these.
- Comma splice: two clauses joined with only a comma — add a conjunction or change to a semicolon.
